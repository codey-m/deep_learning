How to play

The tune writer picks one note at a time. For each pick it lays the possible next notes along a line, likeliest first, each as wide as its chance. The note's luck marks where on the line it lands.

Randomness stretches or squeezes the line before every pick. Pick from the likeliest keeps only that many notes on it.

Write the tune to hear it. Then press any note, or step with the arrows, to see the line it was picked from. Change a dial and the line changes while the luck stays put, so you can see what that note would have been.

Three tune writers learned from different tunes, so their lines look different. Hover or focus a bird to read about it, and press it to switch. Your dials stay as they are.

The riff is the loop the writer falls into, shaded in the tune. A dashed jump is an odd jump, a leap the learned tunes made less than 1 time in 10. Odd notes are hatched on the line.

One note at a time

The tune writer is a next-note model. For each note it holds the chance of every note that could come next, and it writes a tune by picking one note, then the next given that one, and so on. Nothing plans the tune as a whole.

Randomness reshapes the line before each pick, and the luck stays where it is. Turned right down, the favourite takes almost the whole line, so nearly any luck lands on it, and the favourites lead from C to E to G and back to C: the riff. Turned up, the rare leaps get wider, and sooner or later the luck lands on one. For Robin and Wren, randomness alone rarely avoids both, and in the rounds this game picks it never does.

Picking only from the likeliest few notes takes the rare leaps off the line altogether, so the randomness can go up far enough to break the riff without the luck ever landing on a leap.

The right shortlist is however many likely notes the writer's lines hold. Robin's lines hold three likely notes and two rare ones; keep only two and, after C, E or G, both notes left lead back into the riff. Wren's hold two likely notes and three rare ones, so a shortlist of three still holds a rare leap. Lark never leaps, so it needs no shortlist at all, and cutting its lines only takes away ways out of the riff. Too short a list and the tune cannot get away; too long and a leap gets back on.

Language models write text the same way, one token at a time. Their randomness dial is called temperature, and picking from the likeliest k is called top-k sampling. Nucleus sampling, a refinement, sets the shortlist afresh at each step from how the chances are spread.

The tune writer is written for this game. Its table of chances is made up, not learned from real music, and no language model runs. Each pick depends only on the note before it, where a language model looks back over much more. Each note's luck is a fixed random number for the round, so changing a dial changes the line but not the luck. Rounds are chosen so that Robin passes only with a shortlist of three and Wren only with two, at every randomness from 1.4 and 1.2 up. For Lark, shortlists of one and two never pass and keeping every note passes from 1.4 up. About 1 round in 5 qualifies for Robin, 1 in 3 for Wren and more than half for Lark.
